Materials Guide

Choosing your surface

There is no single best countertop — there's the one that matches how you cook, how much maintenance you'll actually do, and what you want to spend.

Granite

Natural igneous stone. Heat-proof, hard to scratch, and no two slabs alike. Slightly porous, so it wants sealing roughly once a year. The workhorse choice, and usually the best value in natural stone.

Quartz (Engineered)

Ground quartz bound in resin. Non-porous, never needs sealing, and the color you see in the showroom is the color you get throughout. Not heat-proof — the resin scorches, so use trivets. The lowest-maintenance surface we install.

Marble

Natural metamorphic stone. Nothing else looks like it, and it stays cool for pastry. It also etches from lemon juice and wine and stains from oil. Choose it knowing it will develop a patina — that's the deal.

Quartzite

Natural, and harder than granite. Often looks like marble but resists etching far better. Dense and difficult to fabricate, which is why it costs more. Excellent for a busy kitchen that wants the marble look.

Porcelain

Ultra-compact sintered slab. UV-stable, heat-proof, stain-proof, and thin. Great outdoors and for waterfall panels. Edges show a color core unless mitered, and it chips if struck hard on a corner.

Butcher Block & Soapstone

The warm options. Soapstone is non-porous, acid-proof, and darkens with mineral oil. Butcher block is inexpensive and repairable but needs regular oiling and hates standing water near the sink.

Thickness

3 cm (1¼") is our standard. It installs directly on cabinets without plywood and gives a substantial edge.

2 cm (¾") costs less and suits bathrooms, but needs a plywood substrate and usually a built-up edge to look right in a kitchen.

Finish

Polished — glossy, reflective, easiest to clean, shows fingerprints on dark stone.

Honed — matte and soft. Hides fingerprints, shows oil more on marble.

Leathered — textured with a slight sheen. Hides everything; our favorite for dark granite.

Seams

Most slabs run about 120" × 65", so a kitchen longer than ten feet gets a seam. We place seams where they're least visible — usually at a sink or an inside corner — and show you exactly where at template. A good seam is felt more than seen, but no seam is invisible.

Overhangs

Up to 10" of unsupported overhang is fine in 3 cm stone. Beyond that you need corbels or steel supports, which we'll spec at template. Standard counters overhang the cabinet face by about 1½".
